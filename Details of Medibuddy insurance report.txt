What the data says

#	Question	Answer
1	Gender?	It barely matters. Males average ₹13,957 vs ₹12,570 for females, but the medians are the same (about ₹9,400). The gap comes from more male smokers, so don't use gender as a constraint.
2	Average spend per policy	Mean ₹13,270, median ₹9,382. Non-smokers average ₹8,434 and smokers ₹32,050.
3	Regional policies?	Not needed. Southeast is highest (₹14,735), but only because it has more smokers and higher BMI.
4	Dependents?	A weak effect. Families with 2–3 children cost a bit more, and the 4–5 children groups are too small to trust.
5	BMI?	It is useful mainly for smokers. Obese smokers average ₹41,558, against ₹19,942 for normal-BMI smokers. For non-smokers BMI hardly matters.
6	Smoker status?	Yes, it is the biggest driver. Smokers are 20% of policies but 49% of total spend.
7	Age?	Cost rises steadily with age, from ₹9,087 (18–25) to ₹18,796 (56–64). It works as a graded loading, not a cut-off.
8	BMI discounts?	Only for non-smokers. A 5% discount on normal-BMI non-smokers costs about ₹67k (0.4% of spend), and 10% costs about ₹134k (0.8%).

My recommendation is to price on smoker × BMI × age band rather than on gender or region.

Machine learning model

I split the data 80/20, used 5-fold cross-validation, and added features driven by the analysis (smoker×BMI, obesity flag, age²). I then tuned Gradient Boosting and Random Forest with randomised search.

Every reasonable model plateaus at about 0.84 R² on the test set (about 0.87 in cross-validation), with a typical error of about ₹2,700. Tuning helped only slightly, and a plain linear model with the engineered features scored about the same. I can't honestly promise higher accuracy from these six fields, because the remaining error most likely comes from things not in the data, such as diagnoses.

7. Important model validation considerations
The notebook uses cross-validation for model selection and an untouched test set for final evaluation. To strengthen the project further before business deployment, evaluate prediction errors separately across age groups, genders, regions, BMI categories and smoking status.

Also consider testing the model on a newer, independent insurance dataset. This dataset contains no policy dates, so temporal validation cannot currently be performed.

Overall conclusion: Smoking status, age and BMI are the most useful predictive variables in this dataset. The tuned Random Forest achieved a test R² of 0.879, making it a strong starting point for an insurance charge prediction project. However, predictive relationships should not automatically be converted into policy restrictions or pricing decisions without further validation.
